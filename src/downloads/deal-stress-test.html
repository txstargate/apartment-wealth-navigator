The Deal Stress Test

For an experienced owner or buyer with an offering memorandum open and a deadline. Print it, tick it, keep it with the file.

Start here: the 10-minute screen

Open the offering memorandum. Answer six questions. Do not call anyone yet. Tick a box when the answer is a flag: it does not match, it is missing, or you cannot tell.

  • The 10 percent test. Cut the seller's NOI by 10 percent. Divide by the asking price. Does the deal still work at that cap rate?
  • Rents. Do the pro forma rents match the rents on the current rent roll?
  • The missing lines. Is vacancy 5 percent or higher? Is there a management fee? Are there reserves?
  • Unit count. Does the rent roll count match the public record and the Certificate of Occupancy?
  • Utilities. Who pays them? One master meter or individual meters?
  • Local rules. In DC, has a TOPA notice started? Is any unit under rent stabilization? Ask an attorney before you rely on the answer.
How to read your answers
  • 0 or 1 flag: the deal earns a full stress test. Request the records (Step 1).
  • 2 or more flags: request the records before you spend more time. Do not write an offer on the seller's numbers.
  • A flag on question 1: the deal only works at the seller's number. It does not work.

How to use the full test

Work Steps 1 to 6 in order. Enter your numbers in the worksheet on page 4, or in the calculator at apartmentwealthnavigator.com/buyernavigator. Compare your stressed value to the asking price. If the offer is due in days: run the 10-minute screen tonight, send the records request tomorrow morning, and run the worksheet the day the records arrive.

Step 1. Request the records

Why: price follows NOI, and NOI is only as good as the documents behind it.

  • T12: trailing 12 months of income and expenses, plus the prior full year
  • Rent roll: current, with unit, rent, lease start and end dates, deposits and concessions
  • Leases for every unit
  • Bank deposit statements for 12 months, to confirm the rent roll is being collected
  • Real estate tax bill and assessment notice
  • Insurance declarations page and claims history
  • Utility bills for 12 months, and a list of which utilities tenants pay
  • Service contracts (trash, elevator, landscaping, pest, laundry) with terms and cancellation clauses
  • Capital improvements over 5 years, open permits, housing code violations
  • Certificate of Occupancy and the public record of unit count
  • Estoppel certificates, if the seller will provide them
  • Local-law notices required before a sale. In DC: tenant purchase rights (TOPA), rent stabilization status and any exemption paperwork, and each unit's rent history
Send this. Copy, paste, fill in the brackets.

[Broker name],

I am reviewing [property address] and want to move quickly. Please send the following: the T12 and the prior full year; the current rent roll with lease dates and deposits; all leases; 12 months of bank deposit statements; the tax bill and assessment notice; the insurance declarations page and claims history; 12 months of utility bills; service contracts; capital improvements over the last 5 years, open permits and code violations; the Certificate of Occupancy; and any estoppels or tenant notices, including TOPA status if the building is in DC.

I will respond within 24 hours of receiving them.

[Your name] [phone]

Step 2. Stress the income

Why: the pro forma shows rents the seller hopes for. The lender uses rents tenants pay.

  • Use rents tenants pay today from the rent roll, not projected market rents
  • Subtract free rent, concessions and tenant credits
  • Compare billed rent to deposited rent (delinquency)
  • Count units against the public record. A rent roll with units the city does not recognize is income that does not exist
  • Underwrite vacancy and credit loss at 5 percent minimum, even if the building is full
  • Count other income (laundry, parking, fees) only if bank records support it
  • Note leases expiring in the next 12 months and any units under rent control limits
  • Underwrite rents at what the law allows, not what the market would pay

Step 3. Reprice every expense as the new owner

Why: the seller's expenses are the seller's. Yours reset the day you close.

  • Taxes: ask how the assessment could change after a sale at your price
  • Insurance: get a current quote for your coverage, not the seller's old premium
  • Utilities: check for a single master meter and for leaks the seller paid out of pocket
  • Management: include a professional fee even if the seller self-manages; get local quotes
  • Payroll and contracts: confirm what you will actually pay to run the building
  • Repairs: compare the T12 to the building's age and condition
  • Reserves: include replacement reserves, commonly $250 to $350 per unit per year; confirm with your lender
  • Value-add: price the renovation, the lost rent during the work, and the inspection surprise before you count the upside

Step 4. Run the financing check

Why: the lender sizes your loan from NOI, not from the asking price. A smaller loan is extra equity you bring.

  • Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) = NOI divided by annual mortgage payments
  • Lenders commonly require 1.20x to 1.25x. Confirm current terms with your lender
  • Maximum annual debt service = stressed NOI divided by required DSCR
  • Maximum loan = maximum annual debt service divided by the loan constant. The loan constant is the year-one payment per $1 of loan. At about 6.5 percent interest and 25-year amortization it is about 0.081. Ask your lender for the real one
  • If the loan you can get is smaller than you planned, the difference is extra equity

Quick rule: at about 6.5 percent interest, 25-year amortization and 1.25x coverage, each $1,000 of NOI supports roughly $10,000 of loan.

Step 5. Price it and size your cash

  • Your value = stressed NOI divided by your target cap rate. Compare it to the asking price. The gap is your negotiating case
  • Cash needed = price minus loan, plus closing costs, plus your reserve
  • Your reserve is the number from your buy box. Write it down before you see the building
  • If your cash needed exceeds what you have, the building fails your buy box, whatever the cap rate says

Step 6. Decide: go, counter, or walk

  • Go: your stressed value is at or above the asking price and the cash fits your reserve rule.
  • Counter: your stressed value is below the asking price. Offer your number. Bring the worksheet line by line.
  • Walk: the seller's price only works at the seller's numbers, or the cash needed breaks your reserve.
  • Whatever the decision, protect it in the contract: contingencies, due diligence period, financing timeline, and the dates your deposit goes hard.

Red flags that need answers before you go further

  • Pro forma rents well above the current rent roll
  • Vacancy below 5 percent, or no vacancy line
  • No management fee, payroll or reserves
  • Rent roll income that does not match bank deposits
  • Unit count that does not match the public record
  • Many leases expiring right after closing, or recent large concessions
  • Seller reluctant to provide the T12, deposits or utility bills
  • A TOPA notice already started, or rent stabilization status unclear (DC)
