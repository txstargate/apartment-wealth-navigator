An owner called me wanting to refinance immediately. He had already called a lender. He delayed 90 days instead, fixed three things, and came back with over $100,000 more in loan proceeds.

You have run the audit. You know where the leaks are. What you do first decides the next year.

Try to fix everything at once and nothing gets done well. Billing cleanup stalls halfway because a vendor rebid is eating the same week. The reporting package never gets built because three other items outranked it every month. Ninety days pass, four things are half done, and the NOI number has not moved.

Three lanes, 30 days each, one at a time.

## Why the lanes have to run in sequence

The fixes have different cycle times, and that is the whole reason the order matters.

Billing cleanup finishes in days. You read the leases, you compare them to the invoices, you correct what is missing. A vendor rebid takes weeks, because you are waiting on three competitors to quote and then waiting on an incumbent to respond. A reporting cadence needs months of data before the variance column means anything. That is why the rebid sits in days 31 to 60 and the reporting sits in days 61 to 90.

Start them together and the slow items set the pace for everything. Run them in order and each lane funds the next one. The fast wins put money in the building before the vendor work starts, and the vendor work produces the clean expense numbers the reporting package needs.

## Lane 1: Days 1 to 30, fast wins

These cost nothing and move quickly.

1. **Billing cleanup.** Audit every active lease and confirm every fee written into it is going out. Late fees, pet rent, parking, storage, utility reimbursements.
2. **Renewal timing.** Calendar every lease expiring in the next 90 to 120 days. Not 30.
3. **Delinquency workflow.** Write down what happens when rent is late, in order, on one page. Then apply that page the same way every month.
4. **Utility triage.** Flag any bill with an unexplained spike and schedule a plumber or a meter review.

You control all four directly. None of them requires a contractor, an approval, or a dollar of capital.

## Lane 2: Days 31 to 60, medium wins

1. **Vendor rebids.** Contact three competitors for each of your top contracts. Use the quotes to renegotiate, whether or not you intend to switch.
2. **Scope tightening.** Read what each vendor actually does against what the contract says they do. The gap between those two is where drift lives.
3. **Turn time system.** Write your make-ready scope. Set a target. Tell the maintenance team both, in writing.

Thirty days is the right window because it matches how long a competitive quote takes. Ask for three bids on day 31 and you will have real numbers to negotiate against by day 50.

## Lane 3: Days 61 to 90, structural wins

1. **Utility recovery.** If your state and your leases allow it, put a billing mechanism in place or repair the one you already run.
2. **Reporting cadence.** Name what the monthly package contains and when it lands. If your manager does not produce one, ask for it in writing.
3. **Rent and renewal strategy.** Set your approach for the next 12 months, unit by unit, against the expiration dates you mapped in lane one.

Structural work goes last for a reason. Utility recovery is worth building once you know what the utilities really cost, and you only know that after lane one flagged the anomalies. Reporting only earns its keep once there is something worth monitoring. Lane three also produces the trailing twelve months a lender will accept at face value.

## What 90 days did to one refinance

Back to that owner. The timing felt right to him.

We mapped his 90-day plan instead. Three items stood out: his collections process was loose, two vendor contracts had drifted well past their original scope, and a utility recovery issue was leaving reimbursements uncollected. All three sat directly on NOI, which meant all three sat directly on his debt service coverage ratio, the number lenders care most about.

He delayed the refinance by 90 days and executed the plan. He came back to lenders with better terms and over $100,000 more in loan proceeds than the original scenario.

Here is the math. At a 1.25 coverage floor and 25-year amortization, every $1,000 of defensible NOI supports roughly $10,000 of borrowing capacity. Run it backwards: $100,000 of extra proceeds is about $10,000 of annual NOI. Collections, two vendor contracts, and one utility recovery fix, and the loan grew by six figures.

The building did not change. What the lender could document did.

**Why does fixing everything at once fail?**
Because the fixes have different cycle times. Billing cleanup finishes in days, a vendor rebid takes weeks of quotes, and a reporting cadence needs months of data to mean anything. Start them together and the slow items stall the fast ones.
