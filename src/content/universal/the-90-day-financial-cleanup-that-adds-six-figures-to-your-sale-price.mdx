Ninety days of work inside your own books, before you list, is the highest-paid work you will ever do as a building owner.

The work does not change the building. The roof is the same roof, and the tenants are the same tenants. It changes what a buyer is willing to believe about the building, and belief is what he pays for.

## Your books were built for the wrong job

Your financials were built for a tax return. They were designed, correctly and legally, to show the least income possible.

Now you want to sell, and you need those same books to show the most income you can defend. Those are opposite jobs. Nobody ever showed you how to present the same books for the second job, because until now you did not need to.

This is where set-and-forget ownership actually costs money. The cost is not in the building. It is in the paperwork nobody has looked at in years.

## Step one: separate personal from property

The car. The phone. The family member on payroll. Travel, meals, and the home office.

Every one of those is a legitimate tax position. Every one of those is also a direct reduction in what a buyer will pay, by the amount of the expense divided by your cap rate, unless you document it as an add back the buyer will accept.

The standard for an add back is simple. You can hand over an invoice and explain it in one sentence.

Undocumented add backs get treated as fiction. Worse, they make the buyer suspicious of everything else in the file. Listing them is not enough. Document them.

Do not delete these expenses from your books. Deleting them creates a mismatch with your tax returns, and the buyer will ask for those returns. Leave them where they are and present them as documented add backs alongside the original statement. Transparency is what makes an add back credible.

## Step two: rent roll integrity

Every unit needs six fields:

1. Current rent
2. Lease start
3. Lease end
4. Deposit held
5. Concessions
6. A signed lease in the file that matches the other five

If your rent roll does not tie to your leases, you will lose credibility on day 10 of due diligence, and you will not get it back. If you want a fast first read, the 15-minute lease check in [How to Prepare a Small Apartment Building for Sale](/insights/how-to-prepare-apartment-building-for-sale/) shows you whether your rent roll has a problem before a buyer finds it.

Pull your bank deposits while you are in there. They have to support the rent roll, because that is the comparison the buyer's analyst runs first.

## Step three: move capital expenditures out of repairs

A roof is not a repair. A boiler is not a repair. If it extends the life of the asset, it is capital, and it does not belong in your operating expenses.

Moving those items out raises NOI legitimately. It is also one of the few adjustments a buyer's lender will accept without an argument, because it is standard accounting, not spin.

The [NOI Leak Audit](/noi-navigator/) is a good place to start sorting your expense lines.

## Fix the file before you paint the lobby

Here is the line I get pushback on. Ninety days inside your books is worth more than $90,000 of renovation, and almost every owner does the work in the opposite order.

They paint the lobby. They redo the entry. They spend real money on things a buyer glances at for four seconds during a walkthrough.

Meanwhile the document the buyer prices from, the one his analyst will spend two weeks inside, has not been touched. Fix the file first. Then paint the lobby if you still want to.

## What the documentation is worth

A typical small building cleanup surfaces about six add backs. They tend to be personal expenses that were never separated, one-time legal or repair costs, and a capital item sitting in the repairs line. Call it $3,500 each.

The arithmetic:

- Six add backs at $3,500 each is $21,000 of NOI.
- At a 6 percent cap rate, $21,000 divided by 0.06 is $350,000.

That is $350,000 of value a buyer can underwrite. You did not renovate anything. You recategorized and documented expenses you had already paid.

The market, the buyers and their lenders set the cap rate. The income they apply it to is the half you control, and documentation is how you prove it.

## The 90-day sequence

The work runs in three blocks of 30 days.

**Days 1 to 30: audit and separate.** Pull 24 months of statements. Flag every personal expense, every one-time cost, and every capital item sitting in the wrong line.

**Days 31 to 60: recategorize and document.** Every add back gets an invoice and a one-sentence explanation.

**Days 61 to 90: rebuild the trailing 12, then stress test it.** Apply 5 percent vacancy. Add a 4 percent management fee. Add reserves.

If the numbers still work after the stress test, you are ready to list. If they do not, you found out now instead of in week three of due diligence.

Do all of this before you talk to a broker, not after. A broker pricing your building off uncleaned financials gives you a low opinion of value, and that number tends to anchor everything that follows.
