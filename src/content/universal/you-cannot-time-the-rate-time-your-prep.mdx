Waiting for rates to come down is not a strategy. It is a hope with a deadline attached.

Owners try to time this market. The ones who come out ahead were ready before the rate showed up, which is a different problem and a solvable one.

## The trap sets itself at 18 months

An owner has a loan maturing in 18 months. The rate on offer looks worse than the one he already has. The plan becomes wait and see.

Two things go wrong.

The first is obvious once you say it out loud. Nobody knows where rates go. Not me, not your lender, not the economists who publish forecasts for a living. You have made your loan outcome depend on a variable no one in the transaction can predict.

The second one is the expensive one. While you wait, the deadline gets closer and your negotiating position gets weaker every month. At 18 months you have time, options, and the ability to walk away from a lender. At 60 days you have a maturity date and whoever will move fastest.

Nothing about the building changed in those 16 months. Everything about your position did.

## What sizes the loan

Your documented income, divided by the coverage requirement, converted at the rate available.

Three inputs. You control the income and how well it is documented. The lender sets the coverage requirement. The rate arrives from a market that does not know your name.

The rate is the one everybody watches. On small buildings the income decides the outcome. Your income statement and the lender's version of it sit further apart than any two rates you will be quoted.

Here is that distance with numbers on it. Take a 12-unit building with $480,000 in gross rents. Vacancy genuinely runs at 3 percent and the owner self-manages, so there is no management fee on his statement. Both facts are true. Neither survives underwriting. The lender applies a 5 percent vacancy factor and adds a management fee at 6 percent of gross rents, which moves NOI from $313,600 to $275,200.

That is $38,400 removed from income. At a 1.25 coverage floor and 25-year amortization, every $1,000 of defensible NOI supports roughly $10,000 of borrowing capacity. So $38,400 times ten is about $384,000 of loan proceeds that never showed up.

No rate produced that number. The gap between two income statements did, and that gap is the piece you can move.

## The timeline that replaces waiting

Four checkpoints. Count backwards from your maturity date.

1. **18 months out. Run your own numbers the way a lender will.** Full-occupancy rent, a 5 percent vacancy factor, normalized expenses, a management fee whether or not you pay one, and replacement reserves. Find your gap. This is the highest-value month in the process, and it costs nothing to run.
2. **12 months out. Fix what you found.** Explain every irregular number in writing. Clean up the documentation. If there is income you can legitimately defend and have not been capturing, capture it now, so it appears in the trailing twelve months the lender will read.
3. **6 months out. Assemble the package and talk to more than one lender.** Financials, operations, property condition, and a one-page narrative. More than one lender is the whole point of doing this early.
4. **90 days out. Execute.** You have options at this point, because you did the work while you still had time to do it.

Step two is why 18 months is the right starting line rather than 12. Income you start capturing today takes roughly a year to fill a trailing twelve. Start at 12 months and the fix is real but the documentation is half-formed when the underwriter opens the file.

## Ready versus waiting, at the same moment

Put two owners side by side at 90 days from maturity.

The first ran the lender's math 15 months ago. She knows her coverage ratio under lender assumptions. Every irregular number in her file has a written explanation attached. She is talking to three lenders and comparing terms, and if one of them prices badly she has two others.

The second started last week. He has one lender, no time, and no way to argue with a management fee he has never paid in his life. He will take what he is given, and he will call it a rate problem afterwards.

Same building. Same market. Same rates. One of them has three quotes and one of them has none.

She will argue the management fee. He will pay for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I wait for interest rates to drop before refinancing?**
Waiting is not a strategy. Nobody knows where rates go, and while you wait your maturity date gets closer and your negotiating position gets weaker every month. Prepare on the income side, which you control, and take the best available outcome in whatever market shows up.

**When should I start preparing to refinance an apartment building?**
Eighteen months before maturity. Run the lender's math at 18 months to find your gap, fix and document it at 12, assemble the package and talk to more than one lender at 6, and execute at 90 days with options already in hand.

**What actually sizes a commercial refinance loan?**
Your documented income divided by the coverage requirement, converted at whatever rate is available. You control the income and the documentation. You do not control the rate, and on small buildings the income side usually costs more than the rate does.
