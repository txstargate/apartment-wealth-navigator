An owner of a 12-unit building told me his rents were maxed out. He was close to right about the rents. He was wrong about the building.

We pulled every active lease. Pet rent sat on three of them and had never been billed. A parking space had been reassigned twice and the invoice never followed. Late fees had not been charged in over a year, because the manager did not want the friction of the phone call.

Correcting those three things added just over $3,000 in annual NOI. No rent increase. No new tenants. No renovation.

## Revenue capture is a billing discipline, not a rent strategy

Revenue capture is collecting everything you are already entitled to under leases you already signed.

Not new income. Not higher rents. The money sits inside an agreement both sides already accepted. The only open question is whether it reaches an invoice every month, on every unit, without exception.

That narrow question is why this lever moves first. Nothing has to be negotiated, approved, or renovated. Someone has to read the leases and check the billing against them.

## Where the money leaks

Five items account for most of it.

1. **Late fees** that go uncharged because the process is inconsistent.
2. **Pet rent** that was never added to the lease, or that quietly stopped being collected.
3. **Parking income** that goes untracked when a tenant switches vehicles.
4. **Utility reimbursements** that were set up once and never reconciled.
5. **NSF fees** waived out of habit rather than policy.

All five share one trait. Each is a small recurring amount no owner would sign off as a write-off if anyone put it in front of him. They vanish because nobody puts them in front of him at all.

The gap runs $2,000 to $5,000 a year per building. Almost none of it comes from a decision anyone made.

## The friction is the reason, not the software

Uncharged late fees are usually a people problem.

Charging a late fee means a harder conversation. Waiving it means no conversation. A busy manager will take the second option, and the second option never appears on a report. Do that for a year and the building has taught its tenants a rule: late is fine.

Inconsistent enforcement trains tenants to pay late. That is the real cost, and it lands well beyond the fee itself, because it lengthens every collection cycle in the building.

## Three fixes you can run this month

1. **Run a lease audit.** Pull every active lease and confirm that every fee, reimbursement, and income item in it is actually billed every month. This step usually finds money inside the first 30 days.
2. **Start renewals 90 to 120 days before expiration, not 30.** Thirty days is not a conversation, it is a deadline. Ninety days leaves room to discuss rent steps while the tenant still has options and no pressure.
3. **Set a delinquency policy and follow it every month without exception.** The policy matters less than the consistency. Pick terms you will actually apply, then apply them the same way in January and in August.

None of the three costs money. All three are calendar and process changes you control directly, which is what separates this lever from every other one in the [NOI Leak Check](/tools/noi-check/).

## What $3,000 a year is actually worth

Spread across 12 units, just over $3,000 a year works out to about $250 per unit per year. Roughly $21 a unit a month.

Twenty-one dollars a unit a month is the size of the leak nobody caught. It is too small to notice on any single statement, which is exactly why it survived.

Now capitalize it. Recovered fee income is recurring, so a lender counts it at refinance and a buyer pays for it at sale. At a 5 percent cap rate, $3,000 of recurring NOI is worth $60,000 in building value. At 7 percent, it is worth about $42,900. The lease audit took an afternoon.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is revenue capture in property management?**
Revenue capture is billing and collecting everything the signed lease already entitles you to. Late fees, pet rent, parking, utility reimbursements, NSF fees. It is not new income and not a rent increase. It is income already agreed to that stops reaching the invoice.

**How much income do owners miss through inconsistent billing?**
Two thousand to five thousand dollars a year per building is the usual range. The money leaks in small recurring amounts, so no single invoice ever looks wrong. One 12-unit building recovered just over $3,000 a year from pet rent, parking, and late fees alone.

**How do I run a lease audit on my building?**
Pull every active lease. List every fee, reimbursement, and income item written into it. Check each one against the last three months of invoices, unit by unit. Anything in the lease that is missing from the invoice is money you are owed. The step usually surfaces something inside 30 days.
