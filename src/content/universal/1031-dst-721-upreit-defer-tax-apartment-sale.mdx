There are three real ways to defer the tax on an apartment building sale. The 1031 exchange is the one owners have heard of. The other two are where the interesting decisions live.

And one of them is a door that does not open again once you walk through it.

**A necessary disclosure first.** This article is general education, not tax, legal or investment advice. Two of these three options involve securities, not real estate. I am a real estate advisor. I can explain how they work. I cannot recommend one to you, and I am not going to. Work through your own situation with your CPA, your attorney, a qualified intermediary, and a securities-licensed advisor.

## Choosing with the clock running

Owners get into trouble by selling first and thinking about deferral second. The moment that sale closes, a 45-day clock starts running.

An owner shopping for replacement property under a 45-day deadline is not a buyer. He is a hostage. He takes whatever is in front of him, from whoever called him first, and he pays for the privilege.

That is passive ownership showing up at the most expensive possible moment. He is choosing under a clock, not on purpose.

Deferral only matters after you have decided that selling is right. If you have not built that comparison yet, start with [Sell, Refinance, or Hold? The 3-Column Math That Ends the Debate](/insights/sell-refinance-or-hold-apartment-building/).

## Option 1: the 1031 exchange

You sell, and instead of taking the money, you roll it into another piece of real property. The tax is deferred, not erased.

The rules that matter:

1. **A qualified intermediary has to be in place before your sale closes.**
2. **You cannot touch the money,** not even for a day.
3. **45 days from closing** to identify replacement property in writing.
4. **180 days total** to close on it.

Then there is the trap: boot. Any cash you walk away with, or any reduction in your debt that you do not replace, is taxable.

Example: sell a $4 million building carrying a $1.5 million loan. You generally need to buy at $4 million or more, with $1.5 million or more of debt, or you create boot.

An illustrative variation on the same sale. Say the replacement building carries a $1.2 million loan instead. The loan drops from $1,500,000 to $1,200,000, and $1,500,000 minus $1,200,000 is a $300,000 reduction in debt. If you do not replace it, that $300,000 is boot, and it is taxable. What boot costs you in tax depends on your own basis and history, so that number comes from your CPA.

What you keep with a 1031 is control, and the ability to do it again.

## Option 2: the Delaware statutory trust

You buy a fractional interest in an institutionally managed property. It qualifies as replacement property for a 1031, which solves two problems at once.

It solves the 45-day deadline, because these are available and ready to close. And it solves the operations problem, because you are done managing anything.

That is appealing to an owner who is tired. It is also why these get sold hard.

What you give up:

- **Control, completely.** You have no say in operations, refinancing, or sale timing.
- **Liquidity, largely.** There is no real secondary market.
- **Flexibility on problems.** The trustee is restricted in what it can do with the property, which means it cannot always react well when something goes wrong.

A DST interest is a security. It is not a deed. You buy it through a broker-dealer, not through me.

## Option 3: the 721 UPREIT

This is the one almost nobody explains to small building owners.

You contribute your property into a real estate investment trust's operating partnership. In exchange, you receive operating partnership units. There is no tax at contribution.

What you get:

- **Diversification** across a whole portfolio instead of one building.
- **An income stream.**
- **Eventual liquidity,** because those units can convert into REIT shares you can sell.

Now the catch. This is a one-way door. Once you hold operating partnership units, you cannot 1031 out again. You are out of real property and into a partnership interest. And when you convert those units into shares, that conversion is a taxable event.

So this is not an exchange strategy. It is an estate and income strategy, and it works best for owners planning around a step-up in basis at death.

## How to choose: four questions, not one

1. **Do you want to keep operating?** If yes, a 1031 into real property. Nothing else keeps you operating.
2. **Do you want out of operations, but want to keep the ability to exchange again later?** That is the DST conversation.
3. **Do you want out permanently, want income and diversification, and are you planning around your estate?** That is the UPREIT conversation.
4. **Are you choosing this, or is somebody selling it to you?** This is the real one.

Here is my line. Deferring tax is not the same as avoiding it. One of these three is a door that locks behind you, and owners are rarely told that before they walk through it.
