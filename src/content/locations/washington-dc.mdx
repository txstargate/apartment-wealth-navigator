In the 12 months to 5 November 2026, 163 apartment buildings of 3 to 20 units sold in Washington DC. Of those, 160 reported a price. They sold at a median of $197,500 per unit, across a range from about $37,500 to $738,000 per unit. That spread is why a citywide average tells an owner almost nothing about a single building.

Each section below links to the full guide behind it.

## The DC market in four numbers

- **Price per unit.** A blended median of $197,500 across 163 sales of 3- to 20-unit buildings in the 12 months to 5 November 2026, up from $192,500 the year before.
- **Cap rate.** Of those 163 sales, only 24 reported a cap rate. The median was 6.95 percent, with the middle half between about 5.8 and 8.1 percent. A thin sample, so a direction only.
- **Vacancy.** About 9.4 percent, up from 9.0 percent (CoStar, 16 November 2026, buildings of 3 to 20 units).
- **Direction.** Buildings of 3 to 20 units now trade as two markets: a median $219,000 per unit for 3 to 4 units and $135,000 for 5 to 20 units. The quality-adjusted index is flat, and sales volume fell 24 percent.

## What moves a DC building's value

- **Building class.** Across the latest sale of each building since 2024, Class B buildings sold at a median of $257,471 per unit, across 62 sales. Class C sold at $191,750, across 238 sales, a gap of about 34 percent.
- **Renovation.** Sales whose records mention a renovation sold at a median of $207,500 per unit, across 35 sales, against $203,417 for the rest. That is about a 2 percent difference, too small to read as a premium.
- **Net operating income.** At a 6.95 percent cap rate, every recurring dollar of NOI adds about $14 of value. Recover $10,000 a year, and the building is worth roughly $144,000 more.

With vacancy rising and rent stabilization limiting covered rents, the expense side is where a DC owner can still add value. ## Three DC rules that change the number

**The Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act (TOPA).** When you sell a covered building, tenants hold a right to purchase first. That adds time and process to a DC sale that a Virginia sale does not carry. Some Maryland counties now have rent control legislation, so the county matters there. Buyers price that timing risk in. Planning the TOPA sequence early protects both your price and your closing date. Read [Selling a DC Apartment Building: TOPA Timing](/insights/selling-dc-apartment-building-topa/)

**Rent stabilization.** Many older DC buildings sit under the District's rent-control rules, which limit how fast rents on covered units can rise. If your rents sit below market in a covered building, you cannot close that gap overnight, and a buyer knows it. That caps the optimistic end of your value range.

**Lender expectations.** A DC lender takes the lower of two tests: about 75 percent of appraised value, and the loan that keeps NOI at roughly 1.25 times the mortgage payment. On small DC buildings the coverage test usually controls, so your defensible NOI decides the number.
