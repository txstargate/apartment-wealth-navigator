A DC apartment building supports a loan sized from the lender's version of net operating income, not the seller's. Take that NOI and divide it by the lender's debt service coverage ratio. Here the ratio is 1.25, so confirm your lender's number. The result is the most annual debt service the building can carry. The lender's rate, amortization and loan structure then turn that payment into a loan amount.

## Key takeaways

- At a 1.25 coverage ratio, maximum annual debt service equals stress-tested NOI divided by 1.25. The mortgage payments cap at 80% of NOI.
- Two extra points of lender vacancy remove 2% of gross potential rent. Expenses do not shrink, so NOI falls by more than 2%.
- The coverage ratio gives you a payment. You need the lender's rate, amortization and structure to turn it into a loan.
- A building can pass 1.25 and still get a smaller loan from the lender's loan-to-value, debt yield, liquidity or property-condition limits.

Buyers of 5 to 50 unit buildings tend to fix a price first and learn the loan second. Then the loan comes back smaller, and the gap becomes extra cash at closing. Lenders work the other way around. They start with NOI, apply their own assumptions, and let the payment limit set the loan.

## How do you turn an offering memorandum's NOI into a maximum loan?

Adjust the stated NOI to the lender's assumptions. Divide by 1.25 to get maximum annual debt service. Then convert that payment into principal using the lender's rate and amortization. The first number you get is a payment. Step 4 turns it into a loan.

### The five steps

1. **Start with the stated NOI** in the offering memorandum. Treat it as the seller's number until you rebuild it.
2. **Recalculate NOI with the lender's vacancy.** Replace the memorandum's vacancy line with the lender's figure, then recompute income, expenses and NOI.
3. **Divide the stress-tested NOI by 1.25.** That is your maximum annual debt service.
4. **Convert the payment into a loan amount.** Ask the lender for the proposed rate, the amortization period, and any interest-only period. Coverage alone cannot give you this number.
5. **Compare the result with the asking price** and with the lender's separate limits. The gap is the equity you must bring beyond your planned down payment.

### Which documents to check first

The memorandum's NOI is only as good as the documents behind it. I read the rent roll, the current leases and the trailing twelve months before I trust any stated NOI. Check:

- the rent roll against the leases, including concessions and delinquency
- the trailing twelve month operating statement, line by line
- utility, property tax and insurance bills against what the statement shows
- capital expenditure history, and any management fee or reserve the seller assumes

I also ask the seller for 12 months of the building's bank statements, to confirm that the rent roll matches what the building actually collected.

Your next lender and your next buyer price the building on the NOI it produces today, from documents they can verify. That is why I stress-test the memorandum before the offer.

## What loan can a building support if the lender adds two points of vacancy?

The loan shrinks by more than two percent, because the vacancy points come off income while expenses stay put. Holding the rate and amortization fixed, the loan falls by the same percentage that NOI falls.

Here is the arithmetic with round, hypothetical numbers. They are not market data and not a forecast for any building. They show the mechanics so you can drop in your own.

| Line (hypothetical building) | Offering memorandum (5% vacancy) | Lender (7% vacancy) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross potential rent | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Vacancy loss | $50,000 | $70,000 |
| Effective gross income | $950,000 | $930,000 |
| Operating expenses | $350,000 | $350,000 |
| NOI | $600,000 | $580,000 |
| Maximum annual debt service at 1.25 | $480,000 | $464,000 |

Two points of vacancy cost $20,000 of income. NOI drops from $600,000 to $580,000, about 3.3%. Divided by 1.25, the supported payment falls by $16,000. At the same rate and amortization, the loan also falls about 3.3%. That is roughly $33,000 for every $1 million of loan the memorandum's NOI would have supported.

Two caveats. Some expenses move with income. A management fee based on collected rent falls a little, so the hit to NOI is smaller than the table shows. And the table stops at the payment. You need a current term sheet to finish the loan amount.

Yes, and for small DC buildings it is light. CoStar puts vacancy for DC buildings of 0 to 20 units at 9.4% as of November 10, against 9.1% for the 4-plus unit market. ## What can still shrink the loan after a 1.25 test passes?

Several separate lender limits can cut the loan below what coverage alone allows.

- **Appraised value.** If the appraisal comes in under your price, the lender lends against the lower number. A DC lender typically takes the lower of two tests. One is about 75% of appraised value. The other is the loan that keeps NOI at roughly 1.25 times the payment. On small DC buildings the coverage test usually controls, as I explain on my [Washington DC page](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/locations/washington-dc/).
- **Debt yield and loan-to-value limits.** These are lender-specific. Get them from a current term sheet.
- **Reserves and escrows.** Required escrows change the cash you need on day one.
- **Borrower liquidity.** In my buy box, I want a cash reserve after closing equal to 15% of the purchase price. That is a conservative figure. On a $1.2 million six-unit building, it is $180,000 after closing.
- **Property condition.** Deferred maintenance can shrink proceeds or add required repairs.

### Is a four-unit building financed like a five-unit building?

No. A building of five or more units is generally underwritten as commercial or multifamily, with a debt coverage test. A building of four units or fewer is generally treated as residential and is typically not subject to the commercial coverage requirement. This post covers buildings of 5 to 50 units. Ask the lender which program applies and what it requires.

### Does the location change the loan math?

The formula does not change, but the timeline can. DC's Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act (TOPA) and rent stabilization rules can affect the sale schedule and the income you underwrite. Virginia does not have TOPA. Maryland rules vary by county.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much can I borrow on a 5 to 50 unit DC apartment building?

It depends on the building's stress-tested NOI, the lender's coverage ratio, and the rate and amortization on the term sheet. Divide the stress-tested NOI by the coverage ratio to find the maximum annual payment. Then ask the lender to convert that payment into a loan amount.

### Does a five-unit building require a commercial loan?

A building of five or more units is generally underwritten as commercial or multifamily, not as one-to-four-unit residential. Confirm the exact program with your lender, because eligibility rules vary by lender and by borrower.

### What happens to the loan if the lender raises vacancy from 5% to 7%?

The loan falls by roughly the same percentage that NOI falls, not by 2%. In the worked table, NOI drops about 3.3%, so at the same rate and amortization the loan drops about 3.3%. Your building's figures will differ.

### Should I use the NOI in the offering memorandum or recalculate it?

Recalculate it. Build NOI from the rent roll, leases and trailing twelve months, then apply the lender's vacancy and expense assumptions. The memorandum's NOI is the seller's starting point, and the lender will not take it at face value.

### Can a building pass a 1.25 coverage test and still be denied?

Yes. Lenders also apply loan-to-value, debt yield, liquidity, reserve and property-condition limits. Any one of them can reduce or block a loan. Passing the coverage test is necessary, not sufficient.
