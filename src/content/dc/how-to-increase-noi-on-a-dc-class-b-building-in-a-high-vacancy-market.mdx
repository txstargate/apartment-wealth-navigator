The levers that raise NOI work the same in Washington DC as anywhere. What changes in DC is which lever you can actually pull. In a Class B building in a high-vacancy market, the rent lever is the one you can pull least.

For the diagnostic behind this page, start with the universal version: [How to Tell If Your Apartment Building Is Underperforming](/insights/how-to-tell-if-your-apartment-building-is-underperforming/). This page puts DC rules and DC numbers behind it, for a Class B building specifically.

## In a high-vacancy market, rent is the wrong first move

Two constraints stack on the rent lever in DC.

Older DC buildings often sit under the District's rent-stabilization rules, which limit how fast rents on covered units can rise. If your rents sit below market in a covered building, you cannot simply reset them and book the gain.

Then vacancy takes the rest. Vacancy in DC sits at about 9.4 percent, up from 8.7 percent a year ago. In a market with that many empty units, a push on asking rent risks the occupancy you already have. When the building next door is offering the same unit, the tenant has options, and you do not get to price as though they do not.

So in a Class B building in this market, the rent lever is capped by law on one side and by vacancy on the other. That moves the work to the expense side and to other income.

## The levers rent stabilization does not touch

Rent stabilization caps the rent. It does not cap any of this.

- **Revenue capture.** Late fees, NSF charges, pet rent, parking, storage, utility reimbursements. Lease-permitted income you are already owed, often not billed consistently.
- **Water and sewer discipline.** The most common silent NOI killer. A single undetected leak or a billing error runs for months before anyone reads the bill closely.
- **Vendor rebids.** Contracts drift and scopes expand. A rate you agreed to in a different market survives into this one through an auto-renewal nobody reads.
- **Turn efficiency.** In a high-vacancy market, slow turns hurt twice, because every extra vacant day competes against a market already soft on absorption.
- **Other income.** Storage, parking, and ancillary services that add income without touching a rent-stabilized rent.

Utility recovery carries its own DC wrinkle. What you can bill back depends on your leases and on DC's rules for master-metered buildings and ratio billing. Confirm what is allowed before you change anything. Once confirmed, it is recurring NOI the rent cap has no say over.

## What the market pays for a Class B building

Operational quality and building class show up in the price per unit a buyer will pay, because a lower-risk building earns a lower cap rate.

In recorded DC sales of 4- to 20-unit buildings from 2024 through July 2026:

- **Class B buildings sold at a median of $244,748 per unit. Class C sold at $191,250.** A gap of about $53,000 per unit, or roughly 28 percent.
- **Renovated buildings sold at a median of $231,250 per unit, against $198,750 for buildings with no recorded renovation.** About a 16 percent difference.

Class and condition are proxies for the same thing: stability and lower operating risk. If you own a Class B building, the market already pays you a premium for it. Losing operational discipline is how a Class B building starts trading like a Class C one, and that slide is worth about $53,000 a unit.

*Source: CoStar, recorded DC multifamily sales, 4 to 20 units, 2024 through July 2026. Market data © CoStar, used with permission.*

## What one point of NOI is worth in DC

DC small buildings that reported a cap rate from 2024 through mid-2026 clustered around a 6.25 percent median. At that cap rate, every recurring dollar of NOI you recover is worth about $16 in value. Recover $10,000 a year, and you have added roughly $160,000 to what a buyer or a lender will assign the building.

That is why the expense and other-income levers matter more in DC than owners expect. When the rent lever is held down by rent stabilization and by vacancy, expense recovery is the value creation you still control, and the market capitalizes it at the same cap rate as any other NOI.

## The DC context, right now

Values are moving against owners, which makes the NOI you control the point of the exercise.

The median price per unit across all small DC buildings, every class combined, has fallen to about $191,894, down roughly 7 percent over the past 12 months. Rent growth has gone flat while vacancy has risen, so the income you do capture is doing more of the work than it used to. Two of the three forces behind that decline, market rent and buyer-required yield, sit outside your control. Your operating income is the one that does not.

## Run it on your own DC building

Take your last 12 months. Walk the six underperformance signals from the [universal article](/insights/how-to-tell-if-your-apartment-building-is-underperforming/), then add the two DC questions:

1. Are any of your units rent-stabilized, and are your current rents at or below the allowable ceiling?
2. Is your utility recovery set up the way your leases and DC rules actually allow, or are you absorbing costs you could bill back?

In a high-vacancy market, the answers tell you where your NOI still moves. For a Class B building, it almost always lives on the expense side and in other income, not in a rent increase you cannot safely make.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I increase NOI on a DC Class B building when vacancy is high and rent control caps my rents?**
Work the levers rent stabilization does not touch: consistent billing of lease-permitted fees, water and sewer discipline, competitive vendor rebids, faster turns, and other income. With rent flat and vacancy high, expense recovery and other income are the NOI you can still control, and the market capitalizes them the same way it capitalizes rent.

**Why is raising rent the wrong first move in a high-vacancy DC market?**
Two reasons stack. Rent stabilization limits how fast covered rents can rise, and high vacancy means a push on asking rent can cost you occupancy you cannot afford to lose. In DC right now, vacancy sits at about 9.4 percent, up from 8.7 percent, so the expense side is the safer place to create value.

**What is a DC Class B building's NOI improvement worth?**
At the roughly 6.25 percent median cap rate seen in recent DC small-building sales, every recurring dollar of NOI adds about $16 of value. A $10,000 annual NOI recovery adds roughly $160,000 to the building's value.

**Why do Class B DC buildings sell for more than Class C?**
A better-classed building carries lower operating risk, so buyers accept a lower cap rate, which lifts the price per unit. In 2024 to 2026 DC sales, Class B sold at a median $244,748 per unit against $191,250 for Class C, a gap of about $53,000 per unit.
