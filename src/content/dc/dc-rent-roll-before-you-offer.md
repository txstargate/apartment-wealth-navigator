# Test a DC Apartment Building's Rent Roll Before You Offer

## How do you test a DC apartment building's income and expenses before you make an offer?

A DC apartment building's stated NOI holds up only if the rent roll matches signed leases and actual bank deposits. The expense column must also match bills, contracts and the District's tax assessment. Test income first. Then rebuild expenses. Then add a capital reserve and downside cases. Base your offer on the NOI that survives those checks, not the one in the offering memorandum.

## Key takeaways

- Northmarq put Washington-area multifamily vacancy at 5.7% in the third quarter of 2026. A regional rate says nothing about whether one building's rent roll is accurate.
- DC taxes Class 1A residential property, including multifamily, at $0.85 per $100 of assessed value. Price taxes from the current assessment and bill.
- The strongest income check is a three-way match: rent roll to signed lease, lease to tenant ledger, and ledger to bank deposits.
- Reserve for recurring replacements separately. A one-time roof or plumbing project is not an operating expense.

The asking price is only the first number in the deal. For a 5 to 50 unit building, the number that matters is the NOI you can verify. Your lender and your next buyer will capitalize that number. Here is how I test it before a client commits.

## How do you check that a DC apartment building's rent roll is real?

Reconcile it three ways: rent roll to signed lease, lease to tenant ledger, and ledger to bank deposits. Any unit where the rent roll shows more than the lease or ledger supports is a flag. A flagged unit is a price conversation.

### Separate in-place income from projected income

For each unit, pull the signed lease, amendments, renewal notices, move-in date, security-deposit record and payment ledger. Compare them line by line against the rent roll the seller handed you.

An offering memorandum often leads with gross potential rent. Treat it as a ceiling. In a small building, one or two vacant apartments move effective occupancy and NOI. Give every unit a status: occupied, vacant, notice given, month-to-month, or down for renovation.

### Reconcile collections, not scheduled rent

Compare scheduled gross rent with what the bank actually received. Ask for 12 months of bank statements. Then account for the gap:

- tenant receivables and write-offs
- concessions and free rent
- employee or owner-occupied units
- vacancy and bad debt
- month-to-month tenancies and notices to vacate

A building can show strong scheduled rent and still collect less than the rent roll total. Separate recently renovated units, units under renovation and units carrying unusual discounts. They do not belong in one blended average.

### Check the rents against the market too

Treat market reports as a sanity check. Northmarq's third-quarter 2026 report put regional asking rents at $2,233 per month. For the District and Suburban Maryland combined, it reported $2,147 per month, down 1.5% year over year.

If a pro forma assumes strong rent growth while those rents are slipping, ask for the renewal and new-lease evidence behind it.

### What records should you request?

| Record | What it tests | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current rent roll | Scheduled rent, unit status, lease dates | Rents that do not match signed leases |
| Leases and amendments | Actual contract rent, concessions, terms | Side agreements or missing amendments |
| Tenant ledgers | What each unit really paid | Chronic arrears or unexplained credits |
| Bank statements (12 months) | Cash received versus scheduled rent | Deposits well below the rent roll total |
| Delinquency and deposit schedules | Collections quality, deposit liabilities | Old balances carried as collectible |
| Operating statements and budget | Expense history and current run rate | Year-to-date results far below prior year |

If these records are slow to arrive, price the delay as risk. I wrote more about timing and sequence in my breakdown of the [45-day due diligence window](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/apartment-building-due-diligence-45-days).

## Which expenses should you rebuild before you capitalize NOI?

Rebuild every recurring line from the latest full operating year, year-to-date results and the seller's current budget. Tie each one to an invoice, bill or contract. The expense column is the part you will control after closing.

### Property taxes

Do not carry forward the seller's historical tax bill. DC states that real property is generally assessed at 100% of market value, per the [DC Office of the Chief Financial Officer](https://cfo.dc.gov/page/tax-rates-and-revenues-property-taxes). The Council sets rates annually. [DC.gov lists Class 1A residential property](https://beta.dc.gov/services/property-taxes), which includes multifamily, at $0.85 per $100 of assessed value.

Use the District's [real property tax database](https://otr.cfo.dc.gov/page/real-property-tax-database-search) to check the assessed value and tax bill independently. It covers more than 200,000 parcels.

### Seller-specific expenses that will not survive closing

Watch for four seller-specific savings: self-management, unpaid family labor, an unusually favorable insurance policy and a related-party contractor. A new owner rarely inherits them. Rebuild management, payroll and insurance on the terms you will have.

Some lines run high instead. Do not accept every line as unavoidable. If a service contract or vendor charge cannot be tied to a record, question it. My [breakdown of NOI levers in a small building](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/how-to-increase-noi-small-apartment-building) shows where the savings sit in a small building.

### Compliance costs

DC apartments must be registered through the rental accommodations process as part of the rental housing business license. The published fee is [$43 per unit for each two-year license period](https://dlcp.dc.gov/service/rental-accommodations-registration-form-details-0), on top of license, application and endorsement fees. Confirm the building's registration is current and that the cost appears in the operating statement.

### Repairs and maintenance versus the physical plant

A low repair line can be good management, or it can be deferred maintenance. During inspections, compare reported repairs with what you see in:

- roofs and masonry
- windows
- plumbing risers and electrical service
- boilers or HVAC
- elevators and life-safety equipment
- common areas

Ask about recurring water-intrusion complaints specifically.

Do not treat a one-time roof replacement as a recurring operating expense. Recurring replacement needs belong in a separate reserve or capital plan.

## What should you do when the numbers do not reconcile?

Reprice off your corrected NOI, ask for the missing records, or walk. Do not split the difference on a number you cannot verify.

### Run downside cases before you offer

Model separate cases for:

- slower lease-up
- higher vacancy
- lower renewal rents
- more concessions
- greater bad debt
- a property-tax change
- an insurance increase
- utility inflation
- higher management costs
- near-term capital work

The asking price should hold up against the NOI that survives those cases. If it only works in the best case, the price is too high. The right adjustment depends on the unit mix, lease status and condition.

### Use market context carefully

Northmarq reported 16 Washington-area multifamily properties trading for $1.8 billion in the third quarter, and a year-to-date median of $297,800 per unit. That region includes Northern Virginia and suburban Maryland, and the trades skew larger than a 5 to 50 unit building. For a closer read on value, see my post on [what a DC apartment building is worth in 2026](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/dc-apartment-building-worth-2026).

### Plan the exit while you buy

Whatever you underwrite today is what a future buyer will test. The records you collect at purchase become the start of your sale package. DC's TOPA rules also shape timing on the sell side, which I cover in [selling a DC apartment building and TOPA timing](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/selling-dc-apartment-building-topa).

Have an attorney review the contract and the lease documents before you commit.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I verify that the rent roll matches the actual leases in a DC apartment building?

Compare every unit's rent roll entry to its signed lease and amendments, then to its payment ledger. Flag any unit where the rent roll shows more than the lease or ledger supports. Finish by matching total ledger payments to bank deposits.

### What documents should I request to test collections before making an offer?

Ask for the current rent roll, all leases and amendments, tenant ledgers, delinquency and security-deposit schedules, operating statements, concession records and 12 months of bank statements. Request utility and tax bills, insurance information, service contracts and repair invoices as well.

### Which operating expenses are most often understated in a seller's pro forma?

Property taxes, insurance, management, payroll, repairs and turnover costs are the usual ones. Owner self-management and deferred maintenance can make these lines look lower than a new owner will experience. Rebuild each line from bills and contracts.

### How should I underwrite property taxes on a multifamily building in Washington, DC?

Start with the current assessment and tax bill, not the seller's historical expense. DC taxes Class 1A residential property, including multifamily, at $0.85 per $100 of assessed value. Check the parcel in the District's real property database and confirm your numbers with your tax advisor.

### What should I do if the rent roll, leases and bank deposits do not reconcile?

Ask the seller to explain each gap in writing, with records. Underwrite the building on the lowest NOI you can verify. If the gaps stay unexplained, reprice the offer or walk away.
