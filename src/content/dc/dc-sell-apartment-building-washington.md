# Selling an Apartment Building in Washington, DC

## How do you sell an apartment building in Washington, DC?

To sell an apartment building in Washington, start with verified documents: a reconciled rent roll, every lease and addendum, and a trailing-12-month operating statement. Then confirm your TOPA category and each unit's rent-stabilization status, because those two items set your timeline and the income a buyer will underwrite. Plan the sale around the income the building produces today, not around a forecast that values will recover.

## Key takeaways

- In my closed-sale data from CoStar and Bright MLS, 186 DC buildings of 1 to 20 units sold in the 12 months to November 5, 2026, against 258 the year before. Fewer buildings sold.
- CoStar puts the market cap rate for DC buildings of 0 to 20 units at 7.3% as of November 10, 2026, up from 7.2%.
- Treat a 4-unit building and a building of five or more units as different compliance categories from the first day of review.
- The rent roll, the leases, the security-deposit ledger and the bank deposits must agree with each other before you market the building.
- No single TOPA timeline fits every building. Confirm the sequence before you advertise or accept a contract.

## What documents does a buyer need to price your apartment building?

A buyer needs a current rent roll, every lease and addendum, a security-deposit ledger, delinquency and collection history, service contracts, repair records, insurance, real-estate-tax bills, licenses, inspection reports and a trailing-12-month (T-12) operating statement. If any of these is missing or inconsistent, the buyer reprices or walks.

If you are selling an apartment building in Washington, build this file by unit before you talk to a single buyer. For each unit, collect:

- The signed lease and every amendment
- Current rent, lawful increases and any concessions
- Deposit amount and ledger
- Payment history and any notices
- Renewal status and utility responsibility
- Any pending tenant claim

Then tie the unit file to the general ledger and the bank deposits. That tie-out is where most small-building files break. My article on [preparing a small apartment building for sale](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/how-to-prepare-apartment-building-for-sale/) covers the full checklist.

### How should you build the trailing 12 months?

Separate recurring operating expenses from one-time capital work, and show both. Buyers need to see real-estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs, maintenance, management, janitorial service, licenses, legal costs, vacancy, concessions and bad debt as separate lines. Strip out anything that belongs to you personally or will not repeat.

The expense column is where I usually find the most room to improve the number, because it is the part you control. My piece on [increasing NOI on a DC apartment building](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/increase-noi-dc-apartment-building/) shows how a change in NOI moves value.

### What happens if the rent roll does not match the leases and deposit records?

The buyer treats the difference as risk and prices it, or uses it to renegotiate during due diligence. A missing lease, unpaid rent that was shown as collected, or a unit that is not on the certificate of occupancy gives a buyer a reason to reopen the deal. Find those gaps yourself, correct them, and disclose what cannot be corrected before an offer, not after. My guide to the [45 days of due diligence](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/apartment-building-due-diligence-45-days/) shows where buyers look first.

## How do TOPA and rent stabilization change the sale of an apartment building in Washington?

They set your sequence and your buyer's risk. TOPA adds procedures that both landlords and tenants must follow, and rent stabilization decides how much income a buyer can underwrite in each unit. Both belong in your plan before you list.

### Is a 4-unit building treated differently from a 5 to 50 unit building?

Treat them as different categories in your first review. The D.C. Department of Housing and Community Development describes [TOPA as part of the Conversion Act framework](https://dhcd.dc.gov/service/conversion-and-sales) governing the conversion, sale and transfer of rental housing. DHCD also states that the Mayor has a purchase right for buildings with five or more rental units when at least 25% of the units are designated affordable. D.C. regulations separately maintain a section for [housing accommodations with five or more units](https://dcregs.dc.gov/Common/DCMR/SectionList.aspx?SectionNumber=14-4715).

Do not assume a small building is exempt. Confirm your unit count, any affordability designation and your TOPA category before you set a marketing or closing schedule.

### When should you map the TOPA sequence?

Map it before you advertise or accept a contract. Have TOPA counsel confirm the notice, offer, election, waiver, contract and closing requirements that apply to your building. DHCD confirms that [landlords and tenants must file and follow procedures](https://dhcd.dc.gov/service/conversion-and-sales), but there is no single timeline I can give you for every building.

A TOPA step discovered after you have an offer runs on the buyer's clock, and every delay becomes a negotiating point. Buyers who know DC price that uncertainty in. I cover the timing side in more detail in [Selling a DC Apartment Building: TOPA Timing and What Buyers Pay a Premium For](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/selling-dc-apartment-building-topa/).

### How does rent stabilization affect what a buyer will pay?

It limits the income a buyer can underwrite in each regulated unit, so the value depends on which units are regulated and which are exempt. For each unit, document whether it is regulated or exempt, the basis for any exemption, the registration or exemption paperwork, the history of increases, and any pending tenant or agency matter.

Do not call a unit exempt because the building is small or because the rent is high. Check your records against the current rule in the D.C. Code and DHCD guidance. A buyer's lender will ask for the same proof.

### Should you wait for the market to recover?

Do not build your plan on a recovery. The median sale price per unit rose 3.1% on paper, yet the quality-adjusted read is flat, and CoStar's market price per unit fell from $209,000 to $201,000. The market is flat to down, and no data here supports a forecast that values will bounce.

Treat timing as an option, not as the core assumption. The better lever is your NOI, and the best time to improve it is before you sell or refinance. If several leases expire soon, underwrite them as the buyer will: what is the renewal status, what is the likely rent, and what does a turnover cost in vacancy and concessions? Show that analysis before the buyer builds a worse one.

### How do assessments and tax history affect valuation?

A buyer may treat an assessment change, an open appeal, an exemption or a tax-payment delinquency as a valuation or closing issue. Pull the current assessment and the tax bills, check for open appeals or payment problems, and reconcile the tax line in your T-12 to the actual bills. Real-estate taxes are one of the largest expense lines, so any mismatch there moves NOI.

### Does the building match its legal use?

Verify the certificate of occupancy or other use authorization, the unit count, zoning status, permits, housing inspection records, and fire and life-safety compliance. Check lead-related records where they apply, and confirm whether any basement or accessory unit is legally recognized. A rent roll that shows more units than the building is authorized to have can affect financing, valuation and your representations to the buyer.

## What order should you follow before you list?

Verify first, structure second, market last. In sequence:

1. **Confirm the category.** Establish your unit count, any affordability designation and your TOPA category.
2. **Build the unit files.** Collect leases, amendments, ledgers, deposits and notices for each unit.
3. **Reconcile.** Tie the rent roll to the general ledger and the bank deposits.
4. **Clean the T-12.** Separate recurring expenses from one-time capital work and owner-specific items.
5. **Resolve the regulatory questions.** Document each unit's regulated or exempt status, and check the certificate of occupancy and permits.
6. **Set the TOPA sequence and the price.** Do this before you sign a listing agreement, then market to buyers who can close on that schedule.

The title company handles settlement and holds the funds at closing, but the work above happens months earlier. Talk to your tax advisor early about an exit strategy such as a 1031 exchange. A qualified intermediary and your CPA carry it out.

The right move depends on your rent roll, leases and regulatory status. I run that with owners one on one.
