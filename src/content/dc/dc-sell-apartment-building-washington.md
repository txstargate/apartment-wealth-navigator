# Selling an Apartment Building in Washington, DC

## How do you sell an apartment building in Washington, DC?

To sell an apartment building in Washington, start with verified documents: a reconciled rent roll, every lease and addendum, and a trailing-12-month operating statement. Then confirm your TOPA category and each unit's rent-stabilization status, because those two items set your timeline and the income a buyer will underwrite. Plan the sale around the income the building produces today, not around a forecast that values will recover.

## Key takeaways

- In my closed-sale data from CoStar and Bright MLS, 186 DC buildings of 1 to 20 units sold in the 12 months to November 5, 2026, against 258 the year before. That is down 27.9%.
- CoStar puts the market cap rate for DC buildings of 0 to 20 units at 7.3% as of November 10, 2026, up from 7.2%.
- Unit count alone does not set your TOPA category. Ownership structure matters for two to four units, and exemptions apply at five or more.
- The rent roll, the leases, the security-deposit ledger and the bank deposits must agree with each other before you market the building.
- No single TOPA timeline fits every building. Planning the sequence before you market the building is good practice, not a statutory deadline.

## What documents does a buyer need to price your apartment building?

A buyer needs a current rent roll, every lease and addendum, a security-deposit ledger, delinquency and collection history, service contracts, repair records, insurance, real-estate-tax bills, licenses, inspection reports and a trailing-12-month (T-12) operating statement. If any of these is missing or inconsistent, the buyer may reprice or walk.

If you are selling an apartment building in Washington, build this file by unit before you talk to a single buyer. For each unit, collect:

- The signed lease and every amendment
- Current rent, lawful increases and any concessions
- Deposit amount and ledger
- Payment history and any notices
- Renewal status and utility responsibility
- Any pending tenant claim

Then tie the unit file to the general ledger and the bank deposits. That tie-out is where most small-building files break. My article on [preparing a small apartment building for sale](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/how-to-prepare-apartment-building-for-sale/) covers the full checklist.

### How should you build the trailing 12 months?

Preserve the actual historical statement as it stands. Do not strip lines out of it. Show recurring operating expenses and one-time capital work separately, and show personal, nonrecurring or normalized items as labeled adjustments with supporting documentation. A buyer then sees both the reported figures and your adjusted view. Buyers need real-estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs, maintenance, management, janitorial service, licenses, legal costs, vacancy, concessions and bad debt as separate lines.

The expense column is where I usually find the most room to improve the number, because it is the part you control. My piece on [increasing NOI on a DC apartment building](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/increase-noi-dc-apartment-building/) shows how a change in NOI moves value.

### What happens if the rent roll does not match the leases and deposit records?

The buyer may treat the difference as risk and price it, or use it to renegotiate during due diligence. A missing lease, unpaid rent that was shown as collected, or a unit that is not on the certificate of occupancy gives a buyer a reason to reopen the deal. Find those gaps yourself, correct them, and disclose what cannot be corrected before an offer, not after. My guide to the [45 days of due diligence](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/apartment-building-due-diligence-45-days/) shows where buyers look first.

## How do TOPA, DOPA and rent stabilization affect a DC apartment-building sale?

They affect both the sale process and the income a buyer can support, but unit count alone does not decide the answer. Each one belongs in your plan before you list.

### Does unit count decide whether TOPA applies?

No. Ownership structure matters for properties with two to four rental units. As of December 31, 2025, and except for two-unit single-family accommodations, full TOPA purchase rights apply to a two-to-four-unit property when a business corporation, as defined in the D.C. Code, owns a majority interest. A two-to-four-unit property not majority-owned by such a business corporation is exempt from the tenant purchase provisions, although tenants keep the notice rights in [§ 42-3404.09(b)](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.09). The rule is in [D.C. Code § 42-3404.10](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.10), subsection (b).

Properties with five or more rental units generally follow the five-or-more-unit TOPA process, but the RENTAL Act added exemptions. These include qualifying new multifamily buildings completed within the preceding 15 years, DCHA properties, certain institutional housing and several specialized ownership transfers. The owner has the burden of establishing an exemption, and an exempt transaction generally requires a Notice of Transfer when no purchase opportunity is provided, with delivery requirements that vary by exemption. See [§ 42-3404.02b](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.02b) and [§ 42-3404.03a](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.03a). The D.C. Department of Housing and Community Development also describes [TOPA as part of the Conversion Act framework](https://dhcd.dc.gov/service/conversion-and-sales) for the sale and transfer of rental housing. The TOPA process for five-or-more-unit accommodations has its own regulation, [14 DCMR § 4715](https://dcregs.dc.gov/Common/DCMR/SectionList.aspx?SectionNumber=14-4715).

### What is DOPA, and how does it differ from TOPA?

DOPA is separate from TOPA. For a covered sale of a housing accommodation with five or more rental units, the owner must provide the District an opportunity to purchase under [D.C. Code § 42-3404.31](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.31). The District's right is subordinate to the tenants' TOPA rights, and the Mayor generally has 30 days after receiving the offer of sale to submit a written statement of interest under [§ 42-3404.32](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.32).

The affordability calculation does not decide whether you must give the District the opportunity. If the District or its assignee buys the property, [§ 42-3404.33](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.33) imposes rent protections, an affordability covenant and an affordability plan. Those requirements use the property's rents, tenant-paid utilities and the applicable median-family-income (MFI) schedule, including a 60% MFI measure for specified units. The DOPA regulations are in [14 DCMR Chapter 24](https://dcregs.dc.gov/Common/DCMR/SectionList.aspx?SectionNumber=14-2401), starting at § 2401. The current [DHCD offer-of-sale form](https://dhcd.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dhcd/publication/attachments/2026-07-15%20-%20TOPA%20DOPA%20Offer%20Of%20Sale%20Form%20-%20CASD.pdf) covers both TOPA and DOPA.

### When should you plan the TOPA sequence?

Plan it before you advertise or ratify a contract with a third-party buyer. That is good practice, not a statutory deadline. DHCD's current form accommodates an offer of sale with or without a ratified third-party contract, and the third party's rights stay conditional on the tenants' rights.

For a sale, the sequence runs through the offer of sale, a statement of interest or tenant-organization registration, negotiation, assignment if any, contract and settlement. For a covered building with five or more units, a newly formed tenant organization generally has 45 days to submit its registration materials, while an existing qualifying organization generally has 30 days. If the organization timely registers, the owner must allow at least 120 days to negotiate a contract. If the tenant organization contracts to purchase, the owner generally must allow at least 120 days after contracting for settlement, subject to statutory extensions. See [§ 42-3404.11](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3404.11). These periods do not all arise automatically if the tenants decline or fail to exercise their rights, so treat them as baseline stages, not a guaranteed total.

Have experienced TOPA counsel determine your category, exemption status, notices, offer-of-sale requirements and closing sequence before you commit to a settlement date. A TOPA step discovered after you have an offer runs on the buyer's clock, and every delay becomes a negotiating point. I cover the timing side in [Selling a DC Apartment Building: TOPA Timing and What Buyers Pay a Premium For](https://apartmentwealthnavigator.com/insights/selling-dc-apartment-building-topa/).

### How does rent stabilization affect what a buyer will pay?

Rent stabilization governs lawful rents and rent adjustments in regulated units, so it affects the income a buyer can reasonably underwrite. Value depends on which units are regulated and which are exempt. Every rental unit must be registered as either regulated or exempt, and an unregistered unit is treated as regulated. See the [DHCD rent-control guidance](https://dhcd.dc.gov/rentcontrol) and [D.C. Code § 42-3502.05](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/42-3502.05).

A building's size or current rent does not, by itself, establish an exemption. The small-owner exemption generally requires four or fewer aggregate rental units, qualifying natural-person ownership, no direct or indirect interest in another District rental unit, and a properly filed exemption claim. "Four or fewer units" alone does not qualify a building. For each unit, document the registration, the exemption basis, the rent history and the lawful increases before you present income to a buyer. A buyer's lender often asks for the same proof.

### Should you wait for the market to recover?

Do not build your plan on a recovery. The indicators point in different directions. Transaction activity and CoStar's modeled price weakened, while the simple median rose. Buildings sold fell 27.9%, dollar volume fell 30.1%, and CoStar's market price per unit fell from $209,000 to $201,000. The median sale price per unit rose 3.1%. No data here supports a forecast that values will bounce.

Treat timing as an option, not as the core assumption. The better lever is your NOI, and the best time to improve it is before you sell or refinance. If several leases expire soon, underwrite them as the buyer will: what is the renewal status, what is the likely rent, and what does a turnover cost in vacancy and concessions? Show that analysis before the buyer builds a worse one.

### How do assessments and tax history affect valuation?

A buyer may treat an assessment change, an open appeal, an exemption or a tax-payment delinquency as a valuation or closing issue. Pull the current assessment and the tax bills, check for open appeals or payment problems, and reconcile the tax line in your T-12 to the actual bills. Real-estate taxes are one of the largest expense lines, so any mismatch there moves NOI.

### Does the building match its legal use?

Verify the [Certificate of Occupancy](https://dob.dc.gov/service/get-certificate-occupancy), the rental-housing [business license](https://dlcp.dc.gov/page/housing-business) and the Rental Accommodations Division (RAD) registration. Then check the unit count, zoning status, permits, housing inspection records, and fire and life-safety compliance. Check lead-related records where they apply, and confirm whether any basement or accessory unit is legally recognized. A rent roll that shows more units than the building is authorized to have can affect financing, valuation and your representations to the buyer.

## What order should you follow before you list?

Verify first, structure second, market last. In sequence:

1. **Confirm the category.** Establish your unit count, your ownership structure, your TOPA category and any DOPA or exemption question.
2. **Build the unit files.** Collect leases, amendments, ledgers, deposits and notices for each unit.
3. **Reconcile.** Tie the rent roll to the general ledger and the bank deposits.
4. **Document the T-12.** Keep the actual statement, and show recurring expenses, one-time capital work and owner-specific items as separate, documented adjustments.
5. **Resolve the regulatory questions.** Document each unit's regulated or exempt status, and check the certificate of occupancy and permits.
6. **Plan the TOPA sequence and the price.** Doing this before you sign a listing agreement is good practice. Then market to buyers who can close on that schedule.

The title company handles settlement and holds the funds at closing, but the work above happens months earlier. Talk to your tax advisor early about an exit strategy such as a deferred 1031 exchange. A qualified intermediary facilitates the exchange, and your tax advisor structures and reports it. The intermediary normally must be engaged before closing, and the 45-day identification and 180-day receipt deadlines then apply.

The right move depends on your rent roll, leases and regulatory status. I run that with owners one on one.

A two-to-four-unit building not majority-owned by such a business corporation is exempt from the tenant purchase provisions under D.C.
