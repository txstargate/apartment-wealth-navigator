Price follows net operating income. The offering memorandum shows the seller's version of it.

Buyer Navigator rebuilds that number from the records, the way a new owner will run the building, before you write an offer. Then it carries the deal through due diligence and closing. I represent buyers and sellers of 4 to 50 unit apartment buildings in Washington DC, Virginia, and Maryland, where I am licensed. This is the buyer's side of the work.

## The engagement, start to finish

1. **Buy box and criteria.** One page of rules that says which deals earn your time: the hours a week you can give the building, the cash you need left after closing, the unit count and submarkets that fit, who pays the utilities, and the return you need for the work. Every listing is checked against it first. If it fails, the answer takes minutes.

2. **Searching and underwriting.** I ask for the T12, the rent roll, every lease, twelve months of bank deposit statements, the tax bill, the insurance declarations page, utility bills, and service contracts. Rents come from what tenants pay today, not from projected rents. Expenses are repriced as the new owner: taxes as they could change after a sale at your price, a current insurance quote, a professional management fee, and replacement reserves. Then the financing check: the lender sizes your loan from NOI, and a smaller loan than you planned is extra equity you bring.

3. **TOPA and rent stabilization, where they apply.** In Washington DC, tenants have purchase rights when a covered building is offered for sale, and some units may be under rent stabilization. I line the TOPA timeline up against your financing and underwrite rents at what the law allows. Virginia does not have TOPA. Some Maryland counties have rent control legislation, so the county matters there. An attorney licensed where the building sits confirms the current rules.

4. **The offer and the letter of intent.** The letter of intent is a short, non-binding outline of the key terms: price, deposit, due diligence length, financing terms, closing date, and the documents you need. A price decides what you pay today. Terms decide who carries the risk for what you have not found yet.

5. **Contract terms and contingencies.** We keep the inspection, financing, and appraisal contingencies. We size the due diligence period to the building. We read every deadline and what happens if someone misses it, and we write down the date your deposit goes hard. Your document requests go into the contract. Your attorney drafts and reviews the legal documents.

6. **Due diligence.** Three tracks run at once. Physical: roof, structure, electrical, plumbing, mechanicals, and the units, with repair estimates and your own inspector. Financial: the T12, the rent roll, bank deposits, and tenant estoppels cross-checked, with expenses rebuilt at your costs. Legal: title, the Certificate of Occupancy, zoning, service contracts, leases, and in DC, TOPA and rent stabilization. Every deadline goes on one page with an owner. We write the walk-away list before you are attached to the deal, and every request to the seller goes in writing, priced off an estimate.

7. **Coordinating the team.** Your lender, inspector, attorney, and title company each own a part. I keep them to one calendar and make sure the title search is ordered early, because title problems take time to clear.

8. **Closing.** The rate lock, the financing and appraisal deadlines, and the closing date stay on the one-page calendar, each with an owner, until the deal closes.

9. **After closing: the 90-day plan.** The written plan for you as the new owner, drafted before you close and handed over after. Days 1 to 30 take control: utilities, insurance, security deposits, rent collection, and a letter to every tenant. Days 31 to 60 reset the costs: re-bid contracts, walk every unit, fund the reserve. Days 61 to 90 measure actual income and expenses against your stress test.

I also look at how you would sell before you buy. If you plan to trade up or consolidate later through a 1031 exchange, a qualified intermediary and your CPA run it. My part is the real estate.
